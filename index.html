Symptoms

You probably recognize at least three of these

None of them look urgent on their own. Together they are the reason your close date keeps sliding and your auditor keeps asking the same question.

  • Month‑end close slips past day ten, every month, and nobody can say exactly which step is the bottleneck.
  • One person maintains the spreadsheet that ties processor payouts to the bank, and everything stops when they take PTO.
  • ACH returns and chargebacks get worked out of an inbox instead of a queue, so aging is invisible until it is large.
  • The bank, the processor and the ledger each report a different number for the same day, and reconciling them is a person, not a control.
  • A reversal posts to the wrong account and gets found six weeks later by accident.
  • Your auditor asks for a control narrative for payment operations and there is nothing written down.
  • Engineering has “reconciliation tooling” on the roadmap for the fourth quarter running.
  • You are about to add a currency, a rail or a partner bank, and nobody wants to be the one who says the current process will not hold.

The audit

What you get in fourteen days

A document your CFO can act on and your engineering lead will not dismiss. Five parts.

Current‑state money map
Every path funds take from customer authorization to settlement to payout, with the system, file and owner at each hop. Card processor reports, BAI2 or MT940 bank files, sponsor bank statements, ledger entries.
Reconciliation and control review
What matches automatically, what a person matches by hand, and what does not match at all. Aging of open breaks, plus where a break could sit undetected and for how long.
Exception inventory
Every exception type you actually generate: returns by code, chargebacks, reversals, duplicate settlements, FX rounding, partial captures. Who works each one, how long it takes, and what happens when that person is out.
Automation shortlist
Ranked by finance hours returned per engineering week spent, so the roadmap conversation stops being a matter of opinion. Includes the items you should not automate yet, and why.
30/60/90 roadmap
Sequenced, with a named owner and a definition of done for each item. Written so it survives my leaving.
What I need from you: read access to one closed month of settlement reports and bank statements, your chart of accounts, and 45 minutes each with the four or five people who touch the process. No production access, no customer PII, no code changes.

Do you need production access?
No. The audit runs on exported files: settlement reports, bank statements, a ledger export, a chart of accounts. No production access, no write access, no customer PII. If your security team wants to review the list before we start, send it to them first.
What if you find nothing?
Then you have a written current‑state map, a control narrative and a documented match rate, which is exactly what your auditor and your next diligence process will ask for and what almost nobody has. That has value on its own. It has also never happened.
Do you work with our engineers or around them?
With them, and the readout is designed for it. Engineering usually knows the reconciliation problem is real and has been unable to get it prioritized against revenue work. A ranked list with hours returned per engineering week is the argument they have been missing.

Because a firm has to staff a team, and the team has to learn your business at your expense before anyone is useful. The scope here is narrow on purpose, I do the work myself, and I would rather be the person you call three times over two years than the invoice you regret once.
